*Bert Vaux (University of Cambridge)*

Artsakh (Classical Armenian *Artsʻakh*, Արցախ) was the tenth of the fifteen provinces of Greater Armenia enumerated in the seventh-century Armenian *Geography* (*Ashkharhatsʻoytsʻ*), lying between Siwnikʻ and Utikʻ, the latter separating it from the river Kura. The name is attested in Armenian from the fifth century, when the *Epic Histories* attributed to Pʻawstos Buzand record it in the variant *Ardzakh* (Արձախ), and Eghishē’s H*istory of Vardan and the Armenian War* in both forms (see below); further medieval forms such as *Ardakh* and *Artsʻakhamar ashkharh* are collected in the standard *Dictionary of Toponyms of Armenia and Adjacent Regions* (Hakobyan et al. 1986). In the eighth century the grammarian Stepʻannos Siwnetsʻi already names an ‘Artsakh’ (*Artsʻakhayin*) dialect among the peripheral varieties of Armenian. Its ultimate origin, however, remains one of the unresolved problems of Armenian toponymy (Hübschmann 1904: 267 = 1907: 102).

### Early attestations
The earliest Armenian attestations have been assembled by Martirosyan (2016). Pʻawstos Buzand twice uses the genitive *Ardzakhay*: the ‘strong district of Ardzakh’ is said to have rebelled against King Arshak (*Apstambetsʻin yArshakay arkʻayēn Hayotsʻ amur gawaṙn Ardzakhay*, 4.50), and the land of Ardzakh is later subdued ‘with a great war’ (*Harkanēr zerkirn Ardzakhay metsaw paterazmaw*, 5.12; Pʻawstos 1883 [1984]: 135, 176). Eghishē mentions the province three times (Eghishē 1989: 190–191, 252–253, 256–257), twice in connection with its forests: *yArtsʻakh* (manuscript variants *yArdzakh*, *yArtsʻakhn*, *yArdzaghn*); *i tʻandzrakhit mayrisn Ardzakhoy* ‘in the dense forests of Ardzakh’ (variants *Ardzakhatsʻ*, *Ardzakha*, *Artsʻakhu*, *Artsʻakhoy*); and *yArdzakhay mayrisn* ‘into the forests of Ardzakh’ (variant *yArtsakha*). Both the long and the short recensions of the *Geography* (*Ashkharhatsʻoytsʻ*) have *Artsʻakh*, genitive *Artsʻakhay*. The one apparent instance of *Ardzakhay* in Soukry’s edition (1881: 33) is a misprint: the manuscript facsimile clearly reads *Arts‘akhay* [Արցախայ] (Hewsen 1994: 51, last line). The form *Artsʻakh* is also found in Movsēs Kaghankatvatsʻi’s *History of the Caucasian Albanians* and in later authors.

### Proposed etymologies
The Greek historian and geographer of the 1st centry BC Strabo (*Geography* 11.14.4) mentions an Armenian district called Orchistene (Gk. Ὀρχιστηνή), which, he reports, supplied the largest contingent of cavalry; this is usually regarded as a Greek rendering of the same name, an identification the *Dictionary of Toponyms* accepts. Several Armenian historians have gone further, identifying Artsakh with the lands called Urteḫe or Urteḫini (and, in some readings, Ardaḫ and Ataḫuni) in the inscriptions of the eighth-century BC kings of Urartu. Hovhannes Karagyozyan (1998), for instance, derives *Artsʻakh*/*Ardzakh* from *Urteḫe*, with Urartian *ḫ* reflected as Armenian *kh*. Martirosyan (2014: 240, 242) leaves open whether this correspondence is acceptable, but notes that Karagyozyan treats the same Urartian sounds differently elsewhere: in deriving Gugarkʻ from Urartian *Uiṭeruḫi* he assumes *ṭ* > *g* and *ḫ* > *kʻ*, whereas *Urteḫe* > *Artsʻakh* would require *ṭ* > *tsʻ*/*dz* and *ḫ* > *kh*. This equation is far from secure. Diakonoff and Kashkai (1981) interpret *Urteḫini* as an inflected form of *Urte*, locate it near the south-eastern shore of Lake Sevan, and suggest that it may denote a mountain rather than a region, while the *Dictionary of Toponyms* dismisses as unlikely the equation of Artsakh with the Urdukhe and Atakhani of the cuneiform texts.

A popular tradition divides the name into *Ar-*, taken to refer to Aran, the legendary ancestor of the region’s princes, and *tsʻakh* ‘brushwood, thicket’, yielding ‘the woods of Aran’. The nineteenth-century scholar Ghewond Alishan cautiously derived the name from *tsʻakh* alone, in allusion to the heavily forested landscape (Alishan 1993) – a landscape to which Eghishē’s ‘dense forests of Ardzakh’ already bear witness. David M. Lang (1988) connected it with Artashēs (Greek Artaxias) I, who incorporated the region into the Armenian kingdom in the early second century BC. Taking the Urartian and Greek forms at face value, Babken Harutʻyunyan (1994) posited an original initial *o-*, on which basis Lusine Margaryan (2020) has proposed \**Ortʻs-akh* ‘place of vines’, from Armenian ortʻ ‘vine’ and a Hurro-Urartian place-name suffix; Margaryan also reviews a derivation from a root *ardz-* ‘rocky, mountainous’. Slava Sargsyan (1996) analyses the name as \**ar-* + \**-di* ‘much’ + \**akh* ‘water’, i.e. ‘abundant in water’, an interpretation Martirosyan (2016) rejects as without foundation.

Most recently, Martirosyan (2016; pers. comm.) has argued that *Artsʻakh*/*Ardzakh* is an Iranian loanword, comparing the Manichaean Middle Persian place name *Arzax* (written *’rzx*), the name of the eastern *kišwar* or ‘clime’, one of the regions into which the Iranian tradition divided the world (Henning 1977: II, 28–29 [orig. 1940]; MacKenzie 1971: 11; Nyberg 1974: 30b, 118–119; Durkin-Meisterernst 2004: 53b). The phonological correspondence is regular: Iranian *rz* is reflected as Armenian *rdz*, as in *ardzak* ‘free, loose’ (*ardzakel* ‘to release’), borrowed from the Iranian root seen in Avestan *harəz-* and Parthian *hyrz* ‘to release, let go’ (Acharyan 1971–1979, I: 324–325; Bolognesi 1960: 50–51; Jahukyan 1987: 517; 2010: 92b; Olsen 1999: 252). On this view the form *Ardzakh*, which predominates in the earliest sources, would be the more original, and *Artsʻakh* a secondary variant with the devoicing and aspiration of stops and affricates commonly found in Armenian varieties across time and space.

### Assessment
None of the earlier proposals satisfy the criteria that Hrach Martirosyan (2010; 2022) has formulated for the etymology of opaque Armenian place names: reliable early attestation, independent confirmation in cuneiform sources, an underlying appellative of secure origin, and semantics appropriate to the place concerned. The *Aran*/*tsʻakh* analysis bears all the hallmarks of folk etymology; the derivation from Artashēs cannot be reconciled with the Armenian form of the king’s name; the ‘vine’ hypothesis rests both on the contested Urartian identification and on an unexplained vowel change; and the ‘abundant in water’ analysis is, as Martirosyan (2016) observes, arbitrary. The fluctuation between *-tsʻ-* and *-dz-* in the earliest Armenian spellings – Pʻawstos has only *Ardzakh*, while the manuscripts of Eghishē waver between the two – together with the divergent Greek form, may indicate that the name was already unanalysable for Armenian speakers in antiquity, and possibly of pre-Armenian origin. Martirosyan’s Iranian etymology offers an explanation of these same facts: if the name was borrowed from Iranian *Arzax*, the *-dz-* forms continue the regular Armenian reflex of Iranian *rz*, and the absence of a native Armenian analysis is expected. The proposal rests on early attestation and a regular sound correspondence with an attested source form, although the semantic link between the name of a cosmographic clime and that of an Armenian frontier province, and the historical circumstances of the borrowing, remain to be clarified. Martirosyan’s studies of the toponymy of Artsakh and Syunik have demonstrated the Armenian origin of a great many local place names; the name of the province itself, however, has resisted a convincing universally accepted explanation. What is beyond dispute is that it has been in continuous Armenian use for more than fifteen centuries.
